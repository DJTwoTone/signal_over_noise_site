# Repurposing Notes — Make the Actor Obvious

Source article:
`/insights/make-the-actor-obvious/`

Core idea:
A presentation sentence becomes harder to follow when the action is clear but the person or team responsible disappears. The practical rule is not “never use passive voice.” It is: **when the actor matters, tell us who it is.**

## LinkedIn

### Hook
A strange thing happens in business presentations:

Decisions get made.  
Changes get implemented.  
Problems get addressed.

And somehow, nobody does any of it.

### Body direction
Use the single “It was decided…” → “The project team decided…” example.

Land on:
> Passive voice is not the enemy. Guessing is.

CTA:
Link to the article or Free Presentation Diagnostic depending on campaign context.

## Email / outreach follow-up

Subject angle:
**Who actually did it?**

Short teaching point:
If a presentation contains several sentences like “it was decided,” “changes were made,” or “testing was conducted,” check whether the audience knows who owns the action. Naming the actor often makes the sentence easier to understand without adding sophisticated English.

## Short video

Hook:
> If your presentation says “It was decided…,” I have one question: who decided?

Structure:
1. Say the vague sentence.
2. Ask “Who decided?”
3. Rewrite it with the actor.
4. Explain that passive voice is fine when the actor does not matter.
5. Close: “Do not fix every passive sentence. Fix the ones that make your audience guess.”

Target length:
30–45 seconds.

## Outreach use

Good follow-up for:
- script-review prospects
- English presentation coaching prospects
- academics or technical teams whose language is formally correct but difficult to process aloud
- anyone whose deck/script has unclear ownership or recommendation language

Do not turn this into a generic grammar lesson.
